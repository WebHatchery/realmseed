# Realmseed

## Game Design Document: Seed Version

## 1. High Concept

**Realmseed** is a fantasy kingdom simulation game about founding settlements in a young, unstable land and watching them grow, compete, fracture, and shape history.

The player begins with a small chartered people entering an unsettled frontier. The world contains fertile valleys, dangerous forests, old roads, river crossings, hillforts, ruins, rival clans, and independent settlements. There is no magic in the seed version. The focus is on people, land, politics, resources, trade, hardship, and memory.

The goal of the seed version is to prove that a small map of settlements can generate meaningful stories through simple but interconnected systems.

---

## 2. Core Fantasy

The player is not managing a single town. They are guiding the birth of a realm.

A village founded beside a river may become the realm’s breadbasket.
A logging camp near the forest may grow rich, resentful, and rebellious.
A frontier fort may save the kingdom during raids, then later demand special rights.
An ignored settlement may fall into ruin and become a warning in the chronicle.

The game should feel like watching a living map become history.

---

## 3. Design Pillars

### 3.1 Settlements Are Characters

Every settlement should feel slightly alive. It has needs, strengths, weaknesses, personality traits, grudges, ambitions, and memories.

A settlement is not just a production node. It is a place with a story.

### 3.2 Simple Systems, Deep Consequences

The seed version should use a small number of stats and resources, but those systems should affect each other often.

Example:

Low food causes unrest.
Unrest reduces loyalty.
Low loyalty increases rebellion risk.
A rebellion weakens trade routes.
Broken trade routes reduce wealth.
Low wealth delays road repairs.
Poor roads make distant settlements feel abandoned.

Tiny gears, big clockwork beast. 🜁

### 3.3 The Map Remembers

The world should preserve important events.

Settlements should remember:

* who founded them
* whether they were neglected
* whether they were saved during crisis
* whether they rebelled
* whether they were conquered
* whether they suffered disasters
* whether they gained special status

The player should finish a campaign with a readable history of what happened.

### 3.4 The Seed Must Stand Alone

The seed version should not feel like an empty shell waiting for future systems. It should be enjoyable without magic, monsters, gods, advanced diplomacy, dynasties, or tactical combat.

The base experience is about founding and surviving as a young realm.

### 3.5 Every Future System Must Attach to the Same Core

Future systems should deepen the same objects rather than replace them.

The permanent foundation is:

* map locations
* settlements
* roads
* factions
* events
* history

---

## 4. Target Experience

The player should regularly think:

* “This site is risky, but it could become powerful.”
* “I need this town, even though it hates me.”
* “Do I support the rich settlement or the loyal one?”
* “Do I fortify the border or feed the interior?”
* “This problem is my fault.”
* “This place has changed so much since I founded it.”
* “That old decision came back to bite me.”

The desired emotional texture is slow-burning, strategic, emergent, and slightly tragic.

---

## 5. Player Role

The player controls the **Charter Council**, the founding authority behind a new realm.

The player does not directly control individual villagers. Instead, they issue high-level decisions:

* where to found settlements
* what each settlement focuses on
* where roads are built
* how resources are allocated
* how events are resolved
* how rival factions are handled
* which settlements receive protection, rights, support, or punishment

The player represents continuity across decades.

---

## 6. Setting: The Greenvale Charter

The seed campaign takes place in **Greenvale**, a frontier region of rivers, forests, hills, old stone roads, abandoned ruins, and contested borderlands.

Greenvale is not empty. It contains:

* scattered independent villages
* rival frontier clans
* abandoned sites
* old forts
* dangerous wilderness
* valuable resource regions
* forgotten landmarks

The player’s people arrive with a royal charter to settle and organise the region. The charter gives legitimacy, but not automatic control.

The land must be won through survival, negotiation, construction, and careful pressure.

---

## 7. Game Structure

### 7.1 Campaign Length

The seed campaign lasts **50 years**.

Each year has 4 seasons:

1. Spring
2. Summer
3. Autumn
4. Winter

Total campaign length: **200 turns**

A shorter prototype version may use:

* 20 years
* 80 turns
* smaller map
* faster settlement growth

### 7.2 Turn Structure

Each season follows this structure:

1. World update
2. Resource production
3. Settlement condition update
4. Faction actions
5. Event generation
6. Player actions
7. Event resolutions
8. Chronicle entry

### 8.1 Seasonal Loop

Each season, the player:

1. Reviews realm condition.
2. Checks settlement warnings.
3. Resolves major events.
4. Chooses development actions.
5. Expands, repairs, negotiates, or stabilises.
6. Ends the season.
7. Watches the world respond.

### 8.2 Long-Term Loop

Over many years, the player:

1. Scouts the region.
2. Founds villages.
3. Connects settlements.
4. Specialises towns.
5. Manages loyalty and stability.
6. Responds to crises.
7. Handles rival factions.

   * Reduce village population.
   * Increase population elsewhere.

4. Blame the local reeve.

   * Gain temporary stability.
   * Reduce loyalty.
   * Chance of future scandal.

Serious Problems
1. The Event System Is Massively Underspecified for Its Role
Events are described as "the primary story system," yet the minimum content target is 44 events. That's catastrophically low for a 200-turn campaign. With seasonal triggers, state-based weighting, and 5–10+ settlements, players will see repeated events within the first 20 turns. The design explicitly wants emergent story and memorable chronicles — that requires hundreds of events, not 44. This is the single biggest gap between the design's ambitions and its content plan.
There's also no system for event sequencing, escalation chains, or follow-up events. "A tax revolt happened" needs a follow-up event next season. Without chained events, everything resets and feels disconnected.
2. Action Economy Is Unresolved
"3 actions per season" is listed as a suggestion, not a design. With 9+ possible action types and 10–24 settlements, 3 actions is almost certainly wrong — either too restrictive (the player can't maintain the realm) or too loose (no real tension). There's no analysis of this. The entire feel of the game pivots on this number and it's left as a placeholder.
3. The Faction AI Has No Real Teeth
Section 13 and 25.2 describe faction behaviour as a list of possible actions with no decision logic. "Rival clan may raid, trade, negotiate, or compete" is not a faction AI — it's a list of verbs. There's no framework for how a rival clan evaluates options, no weights, no goal states, no personality differentiation. With only one rival clan in the seed version, this faction will become predictable within 10 turns and stop feeling threatening.
4. Balance Is Completely Absent
The GDD has no numbers. Not one. No resource production rates, no population growth curves, no loyalty decay rates, no cost values beyond "cheap/moderate/expensive." This isn't a polish problem — without rough numbers, it's impossible to know if the design even functions. Can a single farming village feed itself and a stone camp? How quickly does an ignored settlement drift to rebellion? These aren't details to figure out later; they define whether the systems interact the way the document describes.
5. The Chronicle Is Described as a Feature, Not a System
The chronicle examples in Section 20 are excellent. The problem is the document never explains how the game generates those sentences. What grammar templates exist? How are events tagged for chronicle significance? How does the endgame summary get generated? "The game records important moments" is not a spec. This needs an actual generation system or it will be implemented inconsistently.
6. Victory Conditions Are Vague and Likely Trivial
"Maintain at least 5 controlled settlements" and "keep realm stability above collapse level" after 50 years are very loose win states. A player who passively turtles can likely meet these without ever engaging with the loyalty, faction, or road systems. The design wants players to feel the tension of competing demands, but the victory conditions don't require any of the interesting tradeoffs. There's no reward for playing well, only for not failing.
7. The "Seed Must Stand Alone" Pillar Is Undermined
Section 3.4 says the seed version shouldn't feel like an empty shell. But Section 29 includes extensive "future growth hooks" — ancient sites, sacred places, strange wells, deep forests, bloodlines. Players will notice these flavour elements that go nowhere. A curious player who scouts a "strange well" and gets nothing will feel the absence of the promised depth. Either commit to these hooks being genuinely inert (no flavour text that implies mystery), or cut them until the systems exist to back them up.

Structural Weaknesses
No competitive analysis. The document doesn't acknowledge that Crusader Kings, Dwarf Fortress, Frostpunk, Northgard, and Against the Storm exist. Some of these are direct competitors. Understanding what Realmseed does differently is essential — right now it reads like a lighter CK3 without the dynasty layer, which is a hard sell without a clear differentiator.
No team, timeline, or platform mentioned. This reads like a solo project based on scope, but it's never stated. "Seed version" implies a longer roadmap but there's no indication of what comes after or when.
Prototype milestones are feature lists, not milestones. Milestone 3 is "make geography matter" — that's a design goal, not a deliverable. Real milestones need acceptance criteria: what playable state does this milestone produce?

Bottom Line
The document has a solid conceptual foundation and genuine design thinking in the systems. The cascade chain works on paper. The scope discipline is admirable. But it has a critical gap between concept and specification: no numbers, an event system far too small for its role, placeholder action economy, and unresolved faction AI. As written, this is a strong pitch document and a weak production document. You could show this to get someone excited about the game. You could not build from it without resolving those gaps first.
